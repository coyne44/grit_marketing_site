You graduated with a license and, if you're like most new chiropractors, somewhere north of $200,000 in student debt. Now you want to open your own practice — cash-based, lean, on your terms. The good news: it's more achievable on a small budget than the coaching programs charging you $750/month would like you to believe.

Here's a debt-aware roadmap for the first 90 days.

## The mindset: every expense earns its place

Before the first patient walks in, you'll be pitched a lot of things — fancy build-outs, expensive EHRs, marketing retainers, equipment you don't need yet. The filter is simple: **can the practice survive without this in month one?** If yes, defer it. Cash-flow is oxygen when you're starting from zero, and most "must-haves" are month-six problems.

## Days 1–30: The legal and physical foundation

- **Business entity + EIN.** Form your LLC/PC, get your EIN, open a business bank account. Keep personal and business money separate from day one.
- **Licensing and insurance.** Confirm your state license is active. Get malpractice and general liability coverage.
- **Space.** Start smaller and cheaper than feels comfortable. A modest room you can afford beats a beautiful space that needs 40 patients a week to cover rent. Shared or sublet space is a legitimate way to open.
- **Cash-pay pricing.** Decide your visit price, packages, and any membership model now. Cash-forward means you're not waiting 30–90 days on insurance reimbursement — you get paid at the visit. That's a feature, not a limitation.

## Days 30–60: The systems that run the practice

This is where software comes in — and where new grads tend to either overspend or get stuck. You need a short list:

- **An EHR / practice-management system.** Scheduling, chiropractic SOAP notes, a patient portal, payments. Don't buy a $300/month enterprise system you'll never grow into. Pick something chiropractic-specific, affordable, and fast.
- **Payments.** Card processing for visits and packages. Most modern systems (Grit included) handle this through Stripe.
- **A way to take bookings and intake online.** A patient portal that lets people book, fill out intake, and check in themselves saves you from being your own receptionist.
- **Text reminders.** No-shows are pure lost revenue when you're cash-pay. Automated reminders pay for themselves immediately.

## Days 60–90: Getting patients in the door

- **Google Business Profile.** Free, and the single highest-leverage local listing you'll set up. Fill it out completely.
- **A simple website** with your location, hours, services, prices, and online booking. It doesn't need to be fancy; it needs to load fast and let people book.
- **Your existing network.** If you associated somewhere, tell people you're opening (within whatever your prior agreement allows). Friends, family, your gym, your church — warm referrals are how most solo practices get their first 20 patients.
- **One or two local relationships.** A gym, a CrossFit box, a running store, a massage therapist. Cash-forward patients often come from the places active people already are.

## What you can safely skip in year one

- Insurance credentialing (if you're committed to cash) — it's months of paperwork for reimbursement you've chosen not to chase.
- A large staff. You can run a solo cash practice yourself with good software longer than you'd think.
- Expensive coaching programs. Read, ask questions in chiropractic communities, and reinvest that $750/month into your actual practice.

## The bottom line

A cash practice's whole advantage is low overhead and getting paid at the visit. Protect that. Keep your fixed costs small, choose software that's built for you instead of against you, and put your energy into the only thing that actually grows a practice: taking great care of the patients in front of you.
